🧯Emergency Fund▸

Before you insure anything, invest in anything, or hold a gram of gold, you need a cushion. An emergency fund is money set aside purely to absorb a shock — a job loss, a hospital bill, a broken laptop — without forcing you to sell an investment at a bad time or borrow at a bad rate. In the workshop game, build this stat first; nothing else is stable without it.

Key terms

Emergency fund
Money kept aside, separate from daily spending, sized to cover 3–6 months of essential expenses — not wants.
Liquidity
How fast an asset becomes cash without losing value. A savings account is highly liquid; gold jewellery or a locked-in FD is not.
Essential expenses
The must-pay items: rent, groceries, electricity, insurance premiums, loan EMIs. Eating out and shopping don't count here.
Worked example

Sizing your cushion

If your essential monthly spend is ₹40,000, a 6-month emergency fund target is ₹2,40,000 — sitting somewhere you can withdraw it within a day or two, not locked away.

Where not to keep it: in stocks (they can fall right when you need the cash), in gold jewellery (making charges eat into what you get back), or in a 5-year FD (early-withdrawal penalty). Where it belongs: a savings account, a sweep-in FD, or a liquid mutual fund.

Try it

Add up one month of your family's essential expenses, then multiply by 6. That number is the emergency-fund target.

👛Wallets & Digital Payments▸

"Wallet" used to mean leather and coins. Today most of your money either lives in a bank account or moves through a UPI app — and briefly sits in a digital wallet for small, prepaid spends. The distinction matters more than it looks.

Key terms

Bank account
Where your real money lives, earning some interest, and protected by deposit insurance up to ₹5,00,000 per bank.
UPI (Unified Payments Interface)
India's instant payment rail — it moves money directly between two bank accounts using a phone number, UPI ID, or QR code. No wallet balance involved.
Digital wallet / PPI
An app-based prepaid balance (Paytm Wallet, Amazon Pay Balance) that you top up in advance and spend down. Not a bank account, and usually earns no interest.
PIN / 2FA
The step that authorises a payment. Never share it — not with a stranger, not with someone claiming to call from your bank.
Current limits (2026)

How much can move at once?

Standard UPI payments are capped at ₹1,00,000 per transaction and per day. Specific categories — investing, insurance, education, IPOs, government payments — get a higher ₹5,00,000 cap. UPI Lite handles small, quick payments up to ₹1,000 each, with a ₹5,000 wallet balance, and skips the PIN below a threshold.

A payment app's wallet balance isn't insured the way a bank deposit is. It's fine for daily spends — it's the wrong place to let a large sum sit.

🪙Gold — Physical, Paper & Digital▸

Indian households hold more gold than most countries hold in their entire central bank reserves. But how you hold it today changes what you actually own, and what protection you have if something goes wrong.

Key terms

Physical gold
Jewellery, coins, or bars. Comes with making charges (often 8–25% on jewellery), purity risk, storage/theft risk, and a real gap between buy and sell price.
Sovereign Gold Bond (SGB)
A government bond whose value tracks the gold price, plus a small fixed annual interest. UPDATED No new tranche has been issued since February 2024 — the scheme is effectively paused for new buyers. Existing SGBs can still be traded on the NSE/BSE or held to their 8-year maturity.
Gold ETF
A fund that holds physical gold and trades on the stock exchange like a share. Needs a demat account; tracks the gold price closely with no making charges.
Gold mutual fund
Invests in Gold ETFs on your behalf — no demat account required, and works well for small monthly SIPs.
Digital gold
Buying tiny amounts of gold through an app (Paytm, PhonePe, Google Pay), which claims to store real gold for you.

⚠️ Latest update (November 2025): SEBI has publicly warned that digital gold is an unregulated product — not a security, not a regulated commodity, and outside both SEBI's and the RBI's oversight. If the selling platform fails or acts fraudulently, there is no formal investor-protection or grievance mechanism. That doesn't make it illegal — it means "gold in an app" carries risks a Gold ETF or SGB simply doesn't.

Try it

If gold shows up as an asset in the workshop game, ask yourself: which type am I actually "holding" — and would I be just as comfortable owning the real version?

🔁SIPs — Investing a Little, Often▸

A Systematic Investment Plan (SIP) is a commitment to invest a fixed amount — ₹500, ₹5,000, whatever you choose — into a mutual fund on a fixed date every month, automatically, instead of trying to time one big lump-sum bet.

Key terms

Mutual fund
A pool of money from many investors, professionally managed and invested in a mix of stocks, bonds, or gold, depending on the fund's stated goal.
NAV (Net Asset Value)
The price of one unit of a mutual fund on a given day — you buy "units," not shares.
Rupee-cost averaging
Investing the same amount every month means you automatically buy more units when prices dip and fewer when prices rise — smoothing out volatility over time.
Folio
Your unique account number with a mutual fund house, similar to a bank account number.
Worked example

Twenty years of discipline

A ₹5,000/month SIP into an equity mutual fund, kept up for 20 years, isn't a bet on next month's market — it's a bet on the economy growing over two decades, with the ups and downs averaged out by showing up every month regardless.

Current tax rule (FY2025-26 onward): equity mutual fund gains held over 12 months are taxed at 12.5%, with the first ₹1.25 lakh of gains each year tax-free. Sell within 12 months and gains are taxed at a flat 20%.

📈Stocks — Owning a Piece of a Business▸

A stock (or "share") is a unit of ownership in a company. Buy one share and you technically own a tiny sliver of its factories, brands, and profits — and you gain or lose money as that company's fortunes change.

Key terms

Demat account
An electronic account that holds your shares — mandatory before you can buy or sell stock in India. Think of it as a locker for your investments.
Trading account
Linked to your demat account; this is what you actually use to place buy/sell orders through a broker.
IPO (Initial Public Offering)
The first time a private company sells shares to the public and lists on a stock exchange.
Index (Sensex / Nifty 50)
A basket of top companies used as a scoreboard for the market as a whole — the Sensex tracks 30 major BSE-listed companies, the Nifty 50 tracks 50 major NSE-listed ones.
Dividend
A slice of company profit paid out to shareholders — never guaranteed, and not every company pays one.
Volatility
How much a stock's price swings up and down. Higher potential reward usually rides alongside higher volatility.
Broker
A SEBI-registered platform (Zerodha, Groww, Upstox, and others) that executes your buy/sell orders for a small fee.

Owning one stock is a bet on one company. A mutual fund or index fund spreads that same bet across dozens or hundreds of companies at once — usually the safer place for a first-time investor to start.

Try it

Pick a company whose products you use every day. Is it publicly listed? What would have to go right — or wrong — for its stock price to move?

Everyday Money Skills

🏦Banking & Statements▸

Every rupee that moves through your bank account leaves a trail — a statement. Learning to read one properly is the closest thing to a financial X-ray of your own life.

Key terms

Statement
A complete, dated record of every transaction in an account, from opening to closing balance.
Debit / Credit
Debit = money leaving your account. Credit = money entering it.
Narration
The description on each line explaining who a transaction was with, or why.
IMPS / NEFT / RTGS
Three ways to move money bank-to-bank: IMPS is instant, 24x7, for smaller amounts; NEFT settles in batches through the day; RTGS is for large transfers (₹2 lakh+), settled individually and instantly during banking hours.
Nomination
Naming someone trusted who can access your account if something happens to you — always worth setting up.

Bank deposits — savings account, fixed deposit — are insured up to ₹5,00,000 per depositor, per bank, by the DICGC (a wholly-owned RBI subsidiary). Worth knowing before you decide where your emergency fund lives.

Try it

Sit with a parent/guardian and open one real bank statement. Can you find the opening balance, one debit, and one credit?

⚖️Needs, Wants & Budgeting▸

Every financial decision reduces to one honest question: need, or want? Get comfortable answering it, and budgeting stops being complicated. A budget isn't a punishment — it's just a plan that tells your money what to do before you spend it.

Key terms

Need
Something you must have to function — food, shelter, education, essential transport.
Want
Something enjoyable but skippable — the latest gadget, a night out, a game skin.
Budget
A plan for money built on two numbers: what comes in (income), and what goes out (expenses).
Fixed vs. variable expense
Fixed doesn't change month to month (rent, a school fee instalment). Variable does (electricity, fuel, groceries).
Discretionary spend
Everything left once needs and fixed costs are covered — the part you actually choose.
Zero-based budget
A budgeting style where every rupee of income is assigned a job — spend, save, or invest — before the month starts, so nothing is unaccounted for.
A simple framework

The 50/30/20 rule

One popular way to split take-home income: 50% to needs (rent, groceries, utilities, EMIs), 30% to wants (eating out, entertainment, shopping), and 20% to savings & debt repayment. On a ₹40,000 monthly income, that's ₹20,000 / ₹12,000 / ₹8,000. It's a starting ratio to adjust, not a law — someone with high rent or a big loan may need to shift the split.

The budget categories worth tracking separately

Utilities
Electricity, water, gas, internet, mobile recharge — variable, but rarely optional.
Transportation
Fuel, bus/metro pass, cab rides, vehicle maintenance.
Groceries & essentials
Food, toiletries, household supplies.
Healthcare
Insurance premiums, medicines, doctor visits.
Memberships & subscriptions
Streaming, gym, apps — small individually, easy to lose track of collectively.
Debt payments
EMIs and credit card repayments — a fixed, non-negotiable line every month.
Miscellaneous
Eating out, gifts, one-off purchases, and the genuinely unplanned.

🛡️Insurance▸

Risk can't be avoided — a fire, a theft, a diagnosis. It can only be mitigated. Insurance mitigates the financial half of that risk by pooling small, regular payments from many people to cover the large, rare loss of a few.

Key terms

Policy
The contract listing exactly what an insurer will — and won't — pay for.
Premium
What you pay the insurer, usually yearly, to keep the policy active.
Sum insured / Coverage
The maximum the insurer has promised to pay out.
Claim
Your formal request asking the insurer to pay for a loss.
Co-pay / Deductible
The portion of a claim you pay yourself before the insurer covers the rest — common in health policies, it keeps your premium lower.
Term insurance
Pure life cover for a fixed period, with no maturity payout if you outlive it — usually the most affordable way to protect a family's income.

Rising hospital costs make health insurance one of the least skippable purchases an adult makes — a single major hospitalisation can undo years of savings without it.

💳Loans, Credit & EMIs▸

Borrowing lets you access money now and repay it over time — at a cost. Understanding that cost, and your own track record, decides how good a deal you get.

Key terms

Interest
The lender's fee for letting you use their money.
EMI (Equated Monthly Instalment)
A fixed monthly repayment amount covering part principal, part interest.
CIBIL score
India's most-used credit score, ranging 300–900. Built from your repayment history; 750+ is generally considered good and unlocks better loan rates.
Credit card
The bank pays for your purchase immediately; you repay within a billing cycle or carry a balance forward — at a steep cost.
Down payment
The upfront portion (often ~20% for a home) you pay yourself; the rest becomes the loan.

Unpaid credit card balances typically carry interest of 36–42% a year — among the most expensive borrowing available. A card is a convenience tool for people who clear the bill in full each month, not a way to fund a shortfall.

The Bigger Picture

🧾Income Tax (India, current rules)▸

India's default is now the New Tax Regime — fewer deductions, but lower rates and a rebate that erases tax entirely for most middle incomes. The Old Regime (more deductions, higher rates) still exists if you actively choose it.

Key terms

Financial Year (FY)
India's tax year: 1 April to 31 March, not January–December.
Section 87A rebate
A rebate of up to ₹60,000 that makes tax liability zero for taxable income up to ₹12,00,000 under the New Regime.
Standard deduction
₹75,000 knocked off a salaried person's income automatically — pushes the effective tax-free ceiling to about ₹12.75 lakh for salaried individuals.
Taxable income
Earnings left after allowed deductions — the figure tax is actually calculated on.

📊Inflation & the RBI▸

The same snack costs more each year — that gradual, general rise in prices is inflation. India's central bank, the RBI, has one main lever to manage it: the repo rate.

Key terms

Inflation
A general, ongoing rise in the price of goods and services.
Repo rate
The rate at which the RBI lends short-term money to commercial banks. Raise it, and loans across the economy get costlier — cooling demand and inflation. Cut it, and borrowing gets cheaper, encouraging spending and growth.
Demand-pull vs. cost-push
Prices rise either because more people want something than there's supply of (demand-pull), or because the raw materials to make it got costlier (cost-push).
Hyperinflation / Deflation / Stagflation
Three unhealthy extremes: prices spiralling out of control, prices falling as demand collapses, or prices staying high while growth stalls.

As of late 2026, the RBI's repo rate stands at 5.25%, unchanged since December 2025 — the rate the central bank currently judges enough to keep inflation and growth roughly in balance.

🌱Saving vs Investing▸

Savings and investing solve two different problems. Savings protects money you'll need soon — it should never fall in value. Investing grows money you won't need for years — it can (and will) fall in value sometimes, on the way to growing more than savings ever could.

Key terms

Savings account
Fully liquid, near-zero risk, ~2.5–3.5% p.a. — for money you might need tomorrow.
Recurring Deposit (RD)
Like an FD built month by month — a fixed amount deposited every month for a fixed tenure, at a fixed rate (~6–7%). Good for a goal you're saving towards, like a trip or a gadget.
Fixed Deposit (FD)
A lump sum locked for a chosen tenure at a fixed rate, roughly 6.5–7.5% depending on the bank and term — predictable, but rarely beats inflation by much.
PPF (Public Provident Fund)
Government-backed, 15-year lock-in (with partial withdrawal rules), currently 7.1% p.a., entirely tax-free on maturity — built for long-term, zero-risk goals like retirement.
Debt mutual fund
Invests in bonds and government securities rather than company shares — steadier than equity (~6–8%), but not risk-free and not guaranteed.
Equity mutual fund / SIP
No guaranteed return; long-run historical averages have been roughly 10–12% a year, but with real volatility and real risk of loss in any given year.
NPS (National Pension System)
A government-regulated, market-linked retirement scheme mixing equity and debt, locked until age 60 with limited exceptions — designed specifically to build a retirement corpus.

Real return = your return minus inflation. An FD paying 7% while inflation runs at 5% is only really growing your money's purchasing power by about 2% a year. A "safe" option can still quietly lose ground — which is exactly why long-term goals usually need some equity exposure, not just fixed-rate products.

Worked example

Same ₹10,000, two rates, twenty years

₹10,000 invested once at 7% (roughly a PPF/FD-type rate) becomes about ₹38,700 in 20 years. The same ₹10,000 at 12% (a long-run equity-style average, not a promise) becomes about ₹96,500. The gap isn't the starting amount — it's the rate, compounded, over time. This is why diversification — spreading money across a few of these options rather than betting everything on one — balances that growth against the risk of any single one underperforming.

Rule of 72: a quick way to estimate how long money takes to double — divide 72 by the interest rate. At 12%, money doubles roughly every 6 years (72 ÷ 12). At 6%, it takes about 12 years. Handy for comparing options without a calculator.

🛍️Smart Spending Psychology & Opportunity Cost▸

Classic economics assumed people buy rationally, need by need. Behavioural economics (1980s onward) showed otherwise — mood, marketing, and a well-placed "50% OFF" banner move real money. The single most useful idea in this whole handout might be the next one: opportunity cost — the price you pay isn't just what leaves your wallet, it's what you gave up to spend it.

Key terms

Opportunity cost
What you give up by choosing one option over another. Money spent on one thing is money that can no longer grow, sit safely, or buy something else — that forgone alternative is the real cost, on top of the price tag.
Impulsive purchase
A buy driven by the moment, not a plan — usually the clearest sign that opportunity cost wasn't considered at all.
Anchoring
A pricing tactic where a high "original price" is shown first, so a discounted price feels like a steal even when it isn't one.
Sunk cost fallacy
Continuing to spend on something just because you've already spent on it — a partly-used gym membership, a movie you're not enjoying. Past spending is gone either way; it shouldn't decide the next rupee.
Loss aversion
Losing ₹500 feels roughly twice as painful as gaining ₹500 feels good. Sellers exploit this with "limited time only" and "only 2 left" framing — the fear of missing out outweighs the actual value of the deal.

Newer traps worth knowing

Present bias
Preferring a smaller reward now over a bigger one later — the reason "I'll start saving next month" rarely happens on schedule.
Mental accounting
Treating money differently depending on where it came from — a festival bonus gets spent freely, even though it's exactly as valuable as salary.
Social proof / FOMO
"500 people bought this in the last hour" — using the crowd's behaviour to skip your own evaluation of whether you actually need it.
Buy Now, Pay Later (BNPL)
Splitting a purchase into interest-free instalments. It works directly on present bias — the item feels free today. Missed instalments often carry steep late fees, so treat BNPL as a loan you're taking, not a discount you're getting.
Worked example

Opportunity cost in rupees

A gaming console costs ₹15,000. The obvious cost is ₹15,000. The real cost is what that ₹15,000 could otherwise have become: invested in an equity SIP at a 12% long-run average, it could be worth roughly ₹1,45,000 in 20 years. Buying the console isn't wrong — but the honest question is "is this console worth more to me than ₹1,45,000 someday?", not "can I afford ₹15,000 today?"

The 24-hour rule: for any non-essential purchase above a threshold you set (say, ₹2,000), wait 24 hours — a week for anything above ₹15,000 — before buying. Most impulsive wants quietly lose their pull once the initial excitement fades; the ones that survive the wait were probably worth buying anyway.
